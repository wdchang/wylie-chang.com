Research

My primary line of research concerns shared decision-making in emergency medicine, particularly at the boundaries of the traditional shared decision-making model: encounters in which urgency, psychiatric crisis, uncertain capacity, or disagreement complicate the ordinary process of deliberation between medically reasonable alternatives.

I am interested in how clinicians can establish working relationships that support patients in exercising agency even under these conditions. Building on clinical models of shared decision-making developed by Charles, Elwyn, and others, I draw on Anselm Strauss’s concept of negotiated order and theories of relational autonomy and cooperation to examine how patients and clinicians negotiate trust, authority, and responsibility during acute care.

I have also contributed to research examining emergency medical services and the factors that may produce differences in care and outcomes. I am particularly interested in the effects of system decentralization and the discretion delegated to individual actors—including dispatchers, clinicians, and organizations.

Finally, I am involved in a body of work examining the role of civic engagement and political participation in healthcare settings.
