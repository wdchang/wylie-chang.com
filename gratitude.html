The “Way” of Gratitude: A Daoist Reflection on Awe

Morning Prayers, Appleton Chapel, Harvard University · February 2025

Thank you all for being here this morning. My thanks to Kelley, Rev. Jones, and the seminarians for holding this sacred space. It is an honor to be here with you.

Today’s reading is from the opening line of the Dao De Jing:

道可道，非常道。 Dào kě dào, fēicháng dào. “The Way that can be spoken is not the eternal Way.”

The line begins with a paradox. The Dao—the Way—cannot be fully captured in words, because anything we can name or explain is already smaller than the reality itself. Daoist wisdom therefore asks us to be suspicious of our desire to make everything intelligible: to categorize, measure, justify, and control.

This morning, I want to suggest that gratitude can begin at precisely that boundary.

“Thank you” is something we say every day. Showing appreciation is important in an interdependent society. It acknowledges an exchange. It recognizes that someone has done something for us, and often that they have done it as they should.

Thank you for changing my oil.
Thank you for passing the salt.

There is nothing wrong with this kind of thanks. It is an essential part of how we live together. But I want to distinguish appreciation from another kind of gratitude—one that arises when the logic of exchange breaks down.

Sometimes something happens that we cannot account for.

You don’t know me, and you are in a hurry, but you see that I am lost in a city where I don’t speak the language. You stop, make sure I know where I am going, and then disappear into the crowd. I cannot repay you. You did not ask me to. No one will ever know that it happened.

What do I owe you?

Nothing, perhaps. And yet—

Wow.

Or imagine someone with whom you have been in conflict. You have both carried the tension for some time. And then, unexpectedly, they are the first to offer grace. They decide, without explanation, that the conflict no longer needs to govern the relationship.

You cannot quite explain why they did it. You may not even understand why they were able to let go before you were.

There is nothing to calculate. No debt to settle. No argument to win.

Only:

Thank you.

This, I think, is where the Daoist insight becomes especially powerful. If the Way cannot be reduced to what we can explain, then neither can the deepest parts of our lives. We are shaped by acts of generosity we did not earn, encounters we did not arrange, and circumstances we could never have designed for ourselves.

We often imagine gratitude as a kind of accounting: someone gives, so we should give back. But awe-filled gratitude interrupts that accounting. It recognizes a gift precisely where there is no equivalent return.

And perhaps this is why awe can be so disorienting. Awe is not simply admiration for something impressive. It is the experience of encountering something that exceeds the categories with which we normally make sense of the world. It leaves us, for a moment, without the illusion that everything can be measured against ourselves.

In that sense, gratitude is not merely a feeling of appreciation. It is a way of inhabiting our dependence on a world we did not make.

The Dao De Jing begins by warning us against mistaking our descriptions of the Way for the Way itself. Perhaps gratitude asks something similar of us. We can name what happened. We can tell the story. We can try to explain why someone was kind, why someone forgave us, why we happened to be there at exactly the right moment.

But the explanation is not the experience.

There are moments when the appropriate response to life is not mastery, but receptivity. Not “I understand,” but “I received something I did not expect.” Not “this is what was owed,” but “this was given.”

So let me revisit what I said at the beginning.

Thank you for being here.

Not simply because it is the expected thing to say when one has an audience, but because there is something quietly astonishing about a community gathering here every morning in reverence—across generations, across seasons, across times of peace and turmoil alike. This space exists because people have continued to tend it, and because people continue to arrive.

Thank you to Rev. Jones and the seminarians for holding this space with duty and love, regardless of the color of the day in their lives or ours.

And working as an EMT on ambulances in Cambridge, I am reminded often of the strange contingency of being alive at all. Every ordinary day depends upon an almost incomprehensible chain of circumstances: that we were born, that we survived, that we encountered particular people, that we happened to be here rather than somewhere else.

I cannot make sense of the magnitude of that gift.

Perhaps I do not need to.

The Daoists remind us that there are things beyond the reach of our explanations. Perhaps gratitude is what we do when we reach that edge.

And so, in awe:

Thank you.

Wylie D. C. Chang
10 February 2025
Appleton Chapel, Memorial Church at Harvard University, Cambridge
